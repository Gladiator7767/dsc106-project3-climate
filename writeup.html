Exploring Regional Warming Under Different Climate Scenarios

The visual design and methods are ready for team review. The team development record remains blank and must be completed before final submission.

Question and scope

We aim to compare the temperature difference between low-emission and very high-emission scenarios across different regions and time periods, and then examine whether the pattern remains consistent when switching models. The data are derived from monthly near-surface air temperatures in CMIP6. Historical simulations from 1995–2014 are used to compute baseline values, while SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios are applied for 2015–2100. We selected CanESM5, IPSL-CM6A-LR, and MPI-ESM1-2-LR because these three models contain the required variables and scenarios in the public catalog used in the course. Only one realization (r1i1p1f1) is taken from each model. All six latitudinal regions include both land and ocean. This is a limited comparison and does not represent all climate models.

Preparing comparable temperatures

When calculating the regional average temperature, we apply weighting based on grid-cell area, so smaller grid cells do not carry the same weight as larger ones. For annual averages, each month is weighted according to the number of days in that month within the model's calendar. Then, from each year's value, we subtract the mean annual temperature for the same region and model over the period 1995–2014. Temperature differences are expressed in either Kelvin or Celsius, with identical numerical values. Regions are defined by the latitude of grid-cell centers, so the actual boundaries covered by different models may vary slightly. The global average is calculated directly using the global grid, rather than simply averaging across six regions. Once a time period is selected, each year carries equal weight; when computing the model mean, each model also has equal weight. We do not impute missing values. Complete sources and processing scripts are provided at the bottom of the page.

Visual comparisons

The time series plot uses the horizontal axis to represent years and the vertical axis to show temperature changes. The blue solid line and orange dashed line distinguish between two scenarios. When comparing two time periods, gray hollow circles indicate reference period A, and purple filled diamonds represent current period B. In addition to color, readers can also refer to shape and labels. Each comparison plot uses a common scale; when changing selections, the axis range remains constant to prevent automatic scaling from exaggerating small differences. Maps are used to illustrate the geographical locations of latitude regions and to select areas, but not to represent temperature values. Key numerical comparisons are placed on points and lines, enabling readers to compare positions directly without estimating differences based on map colors.

Scenario gaps and model spread

First, within each model, we subtract the average value of the SSP1-2.6 period from that of the SSP5-8.5 period to obtain the difference between the two scenarios. We then compare how much this difference in period B has changed relative to period A. The calculations are first performed by pairing values from the same model, and then the mean and range across the three models are computed—never subtracting the minimum of one scenario from the maximum of another. The shaded areas and horizontal lines represent the range among the three models, not confidence intervals. The detailed model plots retain all three models and highlight the currently selected one. The trailing ten-year average is used solely to smooth the time series plot and only begins displaying after a full decade. Period comparisons always use the original annual data, so switching smoothing methods does not affect these results.

Comparing periods through interaction

The reference period A defaults to 2021–2040, and the current period B defaults to 2081–2100. Users can modify A, or save the current B as the reference and then select another B by dragging on the timeline chart. Both results will be preserved simultaneously, eliminating the need to remember previous values before switching. When changing regions or models, related charts will update accordingly. Regional comparisons highlight where scenario differences change the most, while model details allow users to check whether different models agree. The page displays start and end years along with the duration of each period, and alerts users if periods overlap or differ in length. The timeline chart also allows switching between temperature increase and scenario difference views. Preset time periods, keyboard controls, reset functions, and a data table and CSV export facilitate further analysis.

Exploration and alternatives

Six initial exploratory plots show time changes, regional differences, scenario gaps, models, and smoothing methods. When a new time period is selected in the first version, the old results are replaced. We retain A and B to allow readers to avoid remembering previous values. We considered plotting lines for all models and regions, but excessive overlap would make comparisons difficult. Comparing fixed regions and periods side by side in small subplots offers a good solution. Our current interaction enables readers to select their own time period, instantly recomputing across all regions before examining different models. We aim for the value of interactivity to lie in addressing these specific questions, rather than in the number of buttons. However, the page still features only one detailed time plot; to compare complete trends across different regions, users must switch between locations.

Reading the results and limits

In this data, the average difference across three models for the Arctic under two scenarios is approximately 0.24°C for 2021–2040 and about 6.61°C for 2081–2100. These values represent outcomes from selected models under two different assumptions, but they do not indicate the likelihood of either future scenario occurring. The warming values are referenced to the period 1995–2014, not pre-industrial temperatures or the temperature thresholds set by the Paris Agreement. Three models, with one realization each, cannot capture all model uncertainties or natural variability. Larger latitudinal regions may also mask internal differences. Shorter or overlapping time periods might yield different comparisons due to annual fluctuations and shared years. These results should not be interpreted as specific predictions for any particular country or city.
